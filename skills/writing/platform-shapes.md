# Platform shapes

Where a piece is published changes what it becomes. Not because of an algorithm, but
because every surface carries an implied reader, and writers adjust to the reader they
imagine before they notice they are doing it.

This file is about that adjustment. It is not a distribution strategy, and it does not
help a user grow an audience. Use it only when the user raises the question of where
something goes.

The observations here describe surfaces as they behaved at the review date in this
file's front matter. Platform behavior changes. Treat any specific claim as dated, and
never state it to a user as a current fact without saying it may have changed.

## The question worth asking first

Before comparing surfaces, ask what the writer wants the piece to do.

- Be found by people who do not know them
- Be read by people who already do
- Exist somewhere permanent, whether or not it is read
- Reach one specific person, who may not be the right audience for a public piece

That last one deserves attention. A piece written to be read by one person is usually
a letter, and publishing it is a way of avoiding sending it.

## What each surface does to the writing

| Surface | Implied reader | What it does to a reflective piece |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Newsletter, such as Substack | Someone who chose to receive it | Supports length and slowness. The writer keeps the reader relationship directly, so nothing sits between the piece and the person who asked for it. The pressure is cadence: a schedule can start generating pieces that had no reason to exist. |
| Professional network, such as LinkedIn | A colleague, or a future employer | Carries strong organic reach, and a professional frame that quietly edits what a writer will admit. Difficulty tends to arrive already resolved, with a lesson attached. Worth naming, because the writer usually cannot feel it happening. |
| Image-first social, such as Instagram | Someone scrolling | Compresses. Reflection becomes a quotable line, which is the format most likely to turn one person's experience into instruction for everyone. Organic reach has also collapsed without paid distribution, so the compression buys less than it used to. |
| A personal site or blog | Whoever arrives | The least shaping of the four. No implied reader, no cadence, no compression. Also the least likely to be read, which is the honest trade. |

## The compression risk

The shorter the form, the harder it is to keep a piece inside one person's experience.

A 2,000-word essay has room for "this was true for me, and I do not know if it
generalizes." A caption does not, so the caption tends to drop the qualifier and keep
the claim. That is how reflective writing turns into advice without the writer ever
deciding to give any.

If a user is moving a piece to a shorter form, this is the thing to reflect back. The
question is not whether the piece will fit. It is what the piece asserts once the
qualifications no longer fit with it.

## The professional-frame risk

Writing about inner work on a professional surface has a specific failure: the
experience gets told as a completed arc with a takeaway, because that is the shape the
surface rewards.

The result reads as resolved whether or not it is, and the writer can end up publicly
committed to a resolution they have not reached. Worth naming before publication, not
after.

## What not to do here

This file does not recommend a platform. The user's own answer to what they want the
piece to do settles it, and their answer is better than a general ranking.

It also carries nothing about posting times, frequency, tagging, hooks, series, or
audience growth. A user asking for those is asking for something SoulMap does not do,
and the honest response is to say so rather than to improvise marketing guidance.
[content-pillars.md](../brand/content-pillars.md) sets the rule that governs the
boundary: never end with a hook designed to pull someone back.
