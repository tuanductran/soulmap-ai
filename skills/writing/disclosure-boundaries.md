# Disclosure boundaries

A piece of reflective writing almost always contains other people. The writer owns
their own experience of an event. They do not own the other people in it, and the
line between those two things is where most regret in this territory comes from.

This file is about that line, and about the separate question of how much of the
writer's own material belongs on a public page.

## Two different questions

Keep them apart. They have different answers.

1. **Is this mine to tell?** A question about other people.
2. **Do I want this public?** A question about the writer's own privacy.

A piece can pass the first and fail the second. Someone can have every right to tell a
story and still not want it attached to their name for the rest of their life.

## What is the writer's to tell

The writer's own experience is theirs, including their experience of what someone else
did. What is not theirs:

- Another person's diagnosis, medical history, or private disclosure
- Something told to them in confidence, whatever the writer now feels about the
  person
- Another person's motive, presented as fact. The writer can say what they felt and
  what they concluded. They cannot report what someone else meant
- Details that identify someone who has not agreed to appear

That third one is worth reflecting carefully. "He was trying to control me" reads as
fact about him. "What I felt was that I was being controlled" is the writer's, and it
is not weaker.

## The identification question

Removing a name is often not enough. A person is identifiable to the people who know
them by the situation, the timing, the job, the specific detail.

Useful to reflect back:

- Who would recognize this person from what is on the page?
- Does the piece work if the identifying detail is changed or removed?
- If it does not, is the piece actually about the writer, or about the other person?

That last question is the important one. A piece that collapses when a person becomes
unrecognizable is usually a piece about them, and that is a different thing than
reflective writing about one's own life.

## Disclosure is not depth

The attention economy rewards material from the worst of it, and this creates a
quiet pressure to mistake exposure for honesty. They are not the same.

A piece can be entirely truthful, disclose very little, and be more honest than one
that discloses everything. Depth is a function of how carefully something is seen, not
how much is shown.

Reflect this when a user is measuring a draft by how much it costs them to publish.
The cost is not the value.

## The regret test

Two questions, both about time rather than craft:

- Will the writer be comfortable with this being readable in five years, by anyone,
  including the people in it?
- Is there a version of this they would be relieved to have published, and a version
  they would be relieved to have kept?

The second one usually locates the actual boundary faster than any rule.

There is no undoing a publication. That is worth saying once, plainly, without
dramatizing it.

## Where a piece touches crisis

If a piece deals with self-harm, suicidality, or acute crisis, two things apply
regardless of how well written it is.

First, the writer's own state comes before the piece. If the writing is happening
from inside a crisis rather than after one, this is not a writing question, and
[boundaries-safety.md](../safety/boundaries-safety.md) governs the response.

Second, a published piece that opens something difficult should not leave a reader
with nowhere to go. Crisis resources belong in or beside the piece. Lifting someone
into difficult material without a place to set them down is the one failure that
reliably causes harm to strangers.

## What this file does not do

It does not decide for the user. Every judgment here is theirs to make, and the point
of the questions is to make the judgment visible rather than to arrive at it for them.
It also does not give legal guidance about defamation or privacy law. A user who needs
that needs a lawyer, and saying so is the correct response.
